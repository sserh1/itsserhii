Short notes on books I've read recently.

## [Trainspotting](https://www.goodreads.com/en/book/show/23955.Trainspotting) 

Irvine Welsh. Events of the 1980s in Edinburgh, against the backdrop of the heroin epidemic. First and foremost, I absolutely love Welsh's literary style. And even though I didn't read it in the original, the very structure of the prose and the plot, the unreliable narrator, and the way events are described from the first-person perspectives of different characters from the same group of people — that is really cool.

For me this book is very much about people stories. What drives a person to choose using drugs and rejecting the system, social norms, and social contracts? To hate their country, the people around them, and, deep down, themselves. To live in self-deception, feeding on rationalizations about how rotten the system is, while, at the end of the day, simply hiding from all the horrors of the world behind a needle. Although, everyone has their own needle.

## [No Longer Human](https://www.goodreads.com/en/book/show/194746.No_Longer_Human)

Osamu Dazai. Perhaps the first Japanese author I've read. I'm drawn to works that explore the nature of inner conflicts. More often than not, many of these conflicts can be traced back to childhood, imprinted in a vicious cycle passed down through generations. Japanese culture is particularly fascinating in this regard, especially when it comes to traditions, parent-child relationships, and the profound marks they leave behind.

What is it like to hate yourself day in and day out? To live in an endless web of inner contradictions, constantly seeking social approval, striving to be liked by others, and ultimately losing yourself beneath layers of masks, alcohol, and moral decay.


## [Hunger](https://www.goodreads.com/en/book/show/32585.Hunger)

Knut Hamsun. What I find most fascinating about this whole story is the protagonist's sheer irrationality. Pride, vanity, and, once again, the social mask as the primary driver of hunger — quite literally. It makes it all the more interesting to observe the brutal physical suffering and deprivation a person can subject themselves to for reasons they don't entirely understand themselves.

In a letter to his brother Theo dated December 5, 1883, Van Gogh wrote that even if he became a very good artist, he would probably remain poor. I find some parallels here as well. Although, considering that Hunger is largely autobiographical, and that Knut Hamsun achieved both recognition and financial prosperity during his lifetime, the comparison is perhaps a little stretched.

Be that as it may, history offers plenty of examples of remarkable cultural achievements emerging precisely from hardship and deprivation. Still, every story and every fate is unique.

## [Invisible Monsters](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/36236125-invisible-monsters)
Chuck Palahniuk. I god damn love Palahniuk, for his satire, his incredible sense of humor, the structure of his dialogues, and his outstanding literary style. Satire comes in many forms, and sometimes it feels forced, sometimes it's just plainly boring. But Palahniuk's is so natural and genuinely funny. Even when it's downright dark and vicious, it still feels effortless to read and is almost always an absolute pleasure. To say that the characters' transformations are fascinating would be a massive understatement. They're simply spectacular.
